\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{F}{unction} computation over multiple-access channels uses the superposition of simultaneous transmissions to evaluate a desired function of distributed observations~\cite{nazer2007,sahin2023survey}. The receiver may need a mean or a variance, for example, without needing the individual observations. Conventional analog over-the-air computation implements this idea through local preprocessing, channel summation, and receiver postprocessing. With amplitude modulation, transmitting scaled real observations makes their sum directly available at the receiver~\cite{channelcomp2024}. For a nonlinear desired function, the central design question is which information each transmitter should encode so that the received sum determines the required value.

Our perspective begins with function-dependent digital modulation, as developed in ChannelComp~\cite{channelcomp2024}. A digital encoder assigns a complex constellation point to each quantized observation. The receiver then observes a sum of constellation points, and different input configurations may produce the same sum. Such a collision is harmless when the desired function has the same value on both configurations; otherwise, the function cannot be recovered even without noise. In the presence of noise, the distances between sums also matter: sums associated with substantially different function values should be sufficiently far apart. Encoder design must therefore address both the information preserved by superposition and the geometry that protects it against disturbances.

To study these questions beyond a particular finite input alphabet, we consider a shared continuous encoder $\enc:I\to\C$, where $I=[-1,1]\subset\R$. As the real observation varies, its transmitted symbol traces a curve in the in-phase and quadrature plane. Restricting this map to a finite alphabet produces a digital constellation, so a continuous curve provides a common design across input resolutions. The continuous formulation also makes a converse question accessible: which encoders can preserve the desired function for \emph{every} input in the interval? Optimality in this continuous class does not, by itself, assert optimality among all constellations on a fixed finite alphabet.

The complex plane is useful here because a single complex symbol has two real coordinates, which can carry two additive statistics. The observations and desired function remain real valued. For variance, an encoder proportional to $s+i s^2$ produces a noiseless sum proportional to $\sum_k s_k+i\sum_k s_k^2$. The receiver obtains the empirical mean $m$ and second raw moment $q$, then evaluates $q-m^2$. This example explains why a curved signal representation arises naturally: each transmitter places its observation and its square in the two signal coordinates. It also raises the main question of this paper. Although these two sums suffice to compute variance, must every shared continuous encoder that identifies variance preserve both sums?

We answer this question for a broader class of real-valued desired functions $f_{\phi,\psi}:I^K\to\R$ of the form
$$
 f_{\phi,\psi}(\mathbf s)
 =\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi(s_k)
 -\psi\!\left(\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_k\right),
 \qquad \mathbf s\in I^K,
$$
where $\phi,\psi:I\to\R$ are prescribed. Variance corresponds to $\phi(s)=\psi(s)=s^2$; other choices include Jensen gaps. Section~\ref{sec:model} gives examples and explains the relation to scalar nomographic representations. We say that the desired function is \emph{identifiable through the encoder} when equal noiseless received sums always imply equal function values. This condition allows input configurations to remain indistinguishable whenever their desired function values agree.

Identifiability is the starting point for robust design. Even when a received sum uniquely determines the desired function, a small disturbance can cause a large output error if unequal function values correspond to nearby sums. We quantify this sensitivity by the smallest squared distance between received sums relative to the squared difference in their function values. Maximizing this \emph{squared-separation score} under a pointwise transmit-power constraint provides a worst-case robustness criterion. Its inverse square root is the smallest uniform disturbance-amplification constant of a decoder that recovers the desired function without noise.

The structural result connects these two parts of the problem. Under the stated assumptions and with at least four transmitters, identifiability forces the encoder to preserve the sums of $s$ and $\phi(s)$ through an invertible real linear transformation. The remaining freedom is geometric: the relative scaling and angle of the two coefficient vectors, together with an offset that places the curve inside the power disk. These choices leave noiseless recoverability intact but change noise sensitivity. Thus the converse reduces an optimization over continuous maps to a finite-dimensional design problem. The input parameterization remains part of the design, since two maps with the same curve image can generate different sums for the same observations.

\subsection{Contributions}
We develop this connection between function identifiability, encoder geometry, and receiver error in three steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Necessary encoder structure.} For $K\ge4$ under Theorem~\ref{thm:rigidity}'s assumptions, we prove that a shared continuous encoder identifies $f_{\phi,\psi}$ if and only if it has the form $\enc(s)=c+us+v\phi(s)$, where $c,u,v\in\C$ and $u,v$ are linearly independent over $\R$. The received sum therefore preserves $S=\sum_k s_k$ and $T=\sum_k\phi(s_k)$. We derive this structure from equal-sum input triples without assuming encoder differentiability, and allow $\phi$ to have affine subintervals.
\item \emph{Optimal geometry and sharp disturbance bounds.} Under Theorem~\ref{thm:sdp}'s stronger assumptions, we express the score through two endpoint gradients and formulate peak-constrained design as a convex program. An equivalent enclosing-ellipse representation connects the power constraint to the geometry of the preprocessing curve. We obtain explicit optimal quadratic and quartic encoders and, for exponential preprocessing, certify a relative global gap below $0.006\%$.

We also extend the characterization and convex design to functions of two prescribed additive statistics under a common-completion identifiability condition. This includes polynomial moment interactions such as $mq$ and a nonpolynomial transformation of variance. For the original function class, a decoder using a fixed linear transformation, scalar clipping, and function evaluation attains the smallest uniform disturbance-amplification constant, including when $u,v$ are nonorthogonal.
\item \emph{A global robustness--accuracy tradeoff.} Worst-case separation need not minimize average error for a given input distribution. Under the stated prior assumptions and for our decoder, we therefore minimize the leading mean-squared error (MSE) coefficient under circular complex Gaussian noise over all shared continuous encoders that identify the desired function and meet a required score. A five-variable convex program attains the optimum and determines a unique $2\times2$ coefficient Gram matrix. The optimal value is continuous and convex in the required score, with an initial plateau and, when nonempty, a strictly increasing branch. For $\phi(s)=s^2$, an explicit scalar-root characterization describes the optimal curve along this tradeoff.
\end{enumerate}
Paired finite-noise experiments and implementation-error bounds connect the analysis to noisy and imperfect transmissions. The appendices give detailed proofs.\ifArxivVersion{} They also establish qualitative approximate rigidity and compare other pairwise criteria.\fi

\subsection{Related Work}
Our formulation draws on computation over multiple-access channels and digital modulation design. Nazer and Gastpar established an information-theoretic framework for computation over multiple-access channels~\cite{nazer2007}; their compute-and-forward construction recovers linear combinations through nested lattice codes~\cite{nazer2011}. Goldenbaum, Boche, and Sta\'nczak combine nomographic representations with nested lattice coding and analyze nonlinear computation rates under a block power constraint~\cite{goldenbaum2015}. Here we jointly encode two prescribed real sums in one complex symbol and study pointwise identifiability and disturbance sensitivity under a power constraint for every input.

Within digital modulation design, ChannelComp imposes separation constraints on received superpositions~\cite{channelcomp2024}. Its finite-input squared-distance objective motivates ours, although its modulation-vector norm constraint differs from our per-symbol energy bound. Variants address multiple antennas, blind federated learning, structured digital coding, and constellation sampling~\cite{veccomp2026,blindfl2024,sumcomp2025,pyramidsampling2025}. Noise-aware ChannelComp adapts the criterion to the noise law~\cite{noiseaware2025}, while prescribed sum-computing grid constellations admit analytical optimization with ML or MAP decoding~\cite{towards2025}. We complement these digital designs by characterizing the continuous encoders that identify the stated nonlinear functions and optimizing their squared separation globally.

The structural question also connects to the mathematical theory of sum representations. Shared sum representations underlie Deep Sets~\cite{zaheer2017}. Wagstaff \emph{et al.}\ establish latent-dimension requirements for universal continuous representation and approximation~\cite{wagstaff19,wagstaff22}. Their exact-max argument combines equal-sum analysis with topology, including fixed cardinality~\cite[Thm. 14 and Lemma 17]{wagstaff22}; particular desired functions may admit smaller representations. Chen, Chen, and Lu use multisymmetric polynomial generators and examine postprocessing singularities~\cite{chen2024}. Our question concerns the structure forced by one prescribed scalar-valued function, rather than a representation shared by all symmetric functions.

For this uniqueness question, the closest affine-uniqueness precedent is Charalambides' restricted Cauchy--Pexider theorem~\cite[Theorem 1.6]{charalambides2013}. For smooth curves without affine subarcs, it identifies affine summands from an additive factorization by regularizing before differentiation. We derive the local factorization from identifiability of a scalar-valued function and permit $C^1$ curves with affine subintervals. We prove this reduction self-containedly; affine uniqueness itself is established. Scalar monotone-generator uniqueness for quasi-arithmetic means provides another precedent~\cite[Theorems A and D]{matkowski2015}. Classical invariance of domain~\cite{hatcher} supplies the topological step in our characterization.

Once the encoder structure is established, the design problem connects to standard convex optimization tools. Our small-noise objective has the weighted inverse-trace form of A-optimal experimental design. Conic formulations with resource constraints are classical~\cite{sagnol2010}, including semidefinite methods for continuous polynomial and rational design spaces~\cite{papp2011}. We apply these matrix and enclosing-ellipsoid tools~\cite{boyd2004} to affine images of $(s,\phi(s))$ inside a peak-power disk. Together with scalar Lipschitz extension~\cite{mcshane1934}, they connect the optimized geometry to uniform error bounds and to the robustness--accuracy tradeoff for our computation class, decoder, and small-noise model.

Section~\ref{sec:pareto} gives the small-noise tradeoff; Sections~\ref{sec:certified} and~\ref{sec:evidence} address numerical certification and performance. The appendices contain proofs and additional consequences.
