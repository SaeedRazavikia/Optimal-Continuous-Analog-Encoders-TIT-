\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{dditive} multiple-access channels combine transmitted signals before the receiver processes them. In over-the-air computation, transmitters encode their local observations so that the receiver can recover a desired aggregate from this superposition~\cite{sahin2023survey}. A sensor network, for example, can report the empirical variance of bounded measurements by transmitting their first and second powers in the two coordinates of one complex symbol. The resulting encoder traces a curve in the complex plane. Its geometry determines both the information available at the receiver and the sensitivity of the computation to noise.


Transmitting the required local features gives an exact construction, but leaves the design problem open. Which features must every exact continuous encoder preserve? How should we scale, translate, and orient the resulting curve under a per-transmitter peak constraint? How much average small-noise accuracy can we gain by relaxing a worst-case robustness guarantee? Answering these questions requires a converse over the continuous encoder class. The input parameterization also matters: two maps with the same geometric image can produce different received sums for the same observations.

We resolve these questions for prescribed targets of the form
$$
 \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi(s_k)-\psi\!\left(\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_k\right),
 \qquad s_k\in[-1,1],
$$
using one shared continuous encoder and one complex channel use. Our objective is squared separation: the minimum squared received distance divided by the squared target difference. Its inverse square root is the smallest uniform factor by which one exact scalar decoder can amplify arbitrary additive disturbances.

\subsection{Contributions}
Our results connect the transmitted symbols, the receiver, and the resulting computation error.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Necessary features.} For $K\ge4$ and the rigidity assumptions in Table~\ref{tab:assumptions}, we prove that every continuous encoder computing the prescribed target exactly is an affine image of $(s,\phi(s))$. In other words, every exact design transmits the same two local features, although their scaling, orientation, and offset may differ. The proof starts from input triples that produce the same channel sum and establishes the conclusion without assuming encoder differentiability. It permits features with affine subintervals.
\item \emph{Exact robustness and peak design.} Under the stated stronger convexity assumptions, we express the exact squared-separation score through two endpoint gradients. We obtain convex encoder-design formulations whose optima are attained and can be converted directly into complex transmit symbols. The inverse-metric form chooses an enclosing ellipse of the feature curve. We solve the quadratic and quartic cases explicitly and certify an exponential example with a global gap below $0.006\%$. A receiver using a fixed linear transformation, scalar clipping, and the target function attains the smallest uniform disturbance-amplification constant, including when the two feature directions are nonorthogonal.
\item \emph{A global robustness--accuracy frontier.} For a specified input distribution and the constructed decoder, we minimize the leading mean-squared error (MSE) coefficient under circular complex Gaussian noise, over all continuous exact encoders meeting a required separation score. A five-variable convex program attains the optimum and determines a unique matrix of feature energies and their inner product, called the feature Gram matrix. The value is continuous and convex, with an initial plateau followed by a strictly increasing branch. Quadratic features admit an explicit scalar-root characterization. This result quantifies how the optimal curve changes when the designer trades uniform robustness for average small-noise accuracy.
\end{enumerate}
Paired finite-noise experiments, deterministic implementation-error bounds, and a qualitative approximate-rigidity consequence support these results. The appendices also treat a restricted polynomial readout extension and distinguish squared separation from other pairwise criteria.

\subsection{Related Work}
Nomographic computation realizes nonlinear functions by preprocessing observations, summing transmitted quantities, and applying a postprocessing map. Goldenbaum, Boche, and Sta\'nczak combine this representation with nested lattice coding and analyze computation rates under a block power constraint~\cite{goldenbaum2015}. We study one complex channel use under a pointwise peak constraint and optimize a prescribed computation's conditioning.


ChannelComp formulates function-dependent modulation through separation constraints on received superpositions~\cite{channelcomp2024}. Extensions and variants address multiple-antenna computation, blind federated learning, structured digital coding, and constellation sampling~\cite{veccomp2026,blindfl2024,sumcomp2025,pyramidsampling2025}. The original finite-input squared-distance formulation provides the antecedent of our objective, but constrains the norm of a finite modulation vector rather than every symbol's energy. Noise-aware ChannelComp uses criteria adapted to the noise distribution~\cite{noiseaware2025}; its Gaussian criterion has an uncentered exponential numerator. Analytical optimization also exists for prescribed sum-computing grid constellations with ML or MAP decoding~\cite{towards2025}. Our result covers all shared continuous planar encoders for the stated nonlinear targets and squared-separation objective.

Shared sum representations underlie Deep Sets~\cite{zaheer2017}. Wagstaff \emph{et al.}\ establish latent-dimension requirements for universal continuous representation and approximation~\cite{wagstaff19,wagstaff22}. Their exact-max argument combines collision analysis with topology; in particular, \cite[Lemma 17]{wagstaff22} treats a fixed cardinality. These results allow particular targets to admit smaller representations. Chen, Chen, and Lu establish representation through multisymmetric polynomial generators and examine readout singularities~\cite{chen2024}.

The closest affine-uniqueness precedent is the restricted Cauchy--Pexider theorem of Charalambides~\cite[Theorem 1.6]{charalambides2013}. For smooth curves without affine subarcs, it identifies affine summands from an additive factorization; its proof regularizes the summands before differentiation. We instead begin with scalar exactness and prove the missing local factorization from a one-way collision implication. Our feature hypothesis also permits $C^1$ curves with affine subintervals. We retain a self-contained proof of this reduction and do not claim affine uniqueness as a new general principle. Scalar monotone-generator uniqueness for quasi-arithmetic means provides a further classical precedent~\cite[Theorems A and D]{matkowski2015}.


The small-noise objective has the weighted inverse-trace form familiar from A-optimal experimental design. Conic formulations for such criteria and additional resource constraints are classical~\cite{sagnol2010}; semidefinite methods also cover continuous polynomial and rational design spaces~\cite{papp2011}. We use these tools after identifying the encoder family forced by exact computation. Our feasible matrices arise from fitting that feature curve into a peak disk. The resulting frontier is global for this computation class and the stated decoder and small-noise model. Matrix optimization and enclosing-ellipsoid principles themselves are established~\cite{boyd2004}.

We use classical invariance of domain~\cite{hatcher} and scalar Lipschitz extension~\cite{mcshane1934}; these tools support the computation-specific reduction and operational interpretation.
